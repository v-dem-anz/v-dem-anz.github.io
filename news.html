To defend democracy in our region, we first need to measure it

November 2026 · Constanza Sanhueza Petrarca and Seraphine F. Maerz

Victorians vote on 28 November, and much of the electoral campaign takes place online. Deepfakes, cloned voices and AI-generated clips are already circulating. As Dr Silvia Montaña-Niño and Prof Andrea Carson write in Pursuit, in one case, a party used a cloned voice to make claims about the opposition leader, in another, a party posted AI-generated clips about migration. Such content is attracting heavy engagement, but whether it will change voters' choices and influence electoral outcomes is hard to know.

Electoral campaigns are short, and democratic change is slow. The effects of a single deepfake may fade within hours or a few days. However, the cumulative effects of a degraded information environment on political trust, political participation and the quality of democracy unfold over years. Capturing them requires evidence collected regularly across countries and over time.

Since 2012, the Varieties of Democracy (V-Dem) project has measured the state of democracy around the world. Over that time, it has grown into one of the most widely consulted sources on democracy for academics, policymakers, civil society and journalists alike. With expertise from more than 4,200 country experts, V-Dem tracks over 600 indicators of democracy in 179 countries, with records stretching back to 1789. The V-Dem dataset is also one of the most comprehensive cross-country measures of democratic quality and includes five core indices which cover electoral, liberal, egalitarian, participatory, and deliberative aspects of democracy.

The latest findings about the state of democracy around the world are worrying. V-Dem's Democracy Report 2026 shows that today 74% of the world population lives under autocratic rule. The remaining 26% live in two kinds of democracy. In 56 so-called electoral democracies, citizens at least choose their leaders in free, fair and competitive elections. Only the remaining 31 liberal democracies go a step further by also constraining leaders once in office, held to account by parliaments and courts, and bound by a rule of law that protects everyone's rights equally. The evidence for East Asia and the Pacific region points to an even more concerning trend: only 11% of the region's population lives in democracies such as Australia, Japan and New Zealand. The remaining 89% live under autocratic rule.

How can democracy endure? How can we strengthen it in our region? And how can it meet the new challenges it faces? These are the questions at the heart of the new V-Dem Regional Centre for Australia and New Zealand, co-founded by Dr Seraphine Maerz of the University of Melbourne and Dr Constanza Sanhueza Petrarca from the Australian National University. The Centre will bring together researchers, policymakers and practitioners, journalists and civil society from across the region to engage in measuring democracy in the region, bringing V-Dem's approach "Global Standards, Local Knowledge" to practice. The new Regional Centre joins 9 other regional centres from around the world, and links to the V-Dem Institute at the University of Gothenburg and a global network of democracy experts.

To kick-start the conversation, the founding directors will host a free public panel at the University of Melbourne's Old Arts Hall, "Democratic resilience in the Age of AI", on Wednesday 9 December from 4:00 to 5:30 pm, followed by a reception. The panel will be joined by Professor Steven Wilson (Director of the V-Dem Institute), Professor Carl-Henrik Knutsen (University of Oslo and V-Dem Principal Investigator), Professor Andrea Carson (University of Melbourne) and Professor Bec Strating (La Trobe University). This event will open a series of activities through 2027, including roundtables, workshops, policy briefs and reports.

Defending democracy depends on knowing where it remains strong and where it is beginning to erode. The new network helps to ensure that these important debates rely on robust empirical evidence. We invite researchers, journalists, public servants and anyone interested in the future of democracy in our region to join us on 9 December.

Seraphine F. Maerz is a Senior Lecturer at the University of Melbourne, Constanza Sanhueza Petrarca is a Senior Lecturer at the Australian National University. They are the Directors of the new V-Dem Regional Centre for Australia and New Zealand and Project Managers of the V-Dem project.
